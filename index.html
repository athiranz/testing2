The Last Bloom

— A promise made by two children in a church shelter, twenty years ago

Section I

The World of Viremont

Viremont is a city that has learned to smile over its graves. It sits in a river valley where morning fog rolls in thick and unhurried, swallowing church towers before noon. Beautiful the way old grief is beautiful — you can't look at it too long without feeling something you can't name.

Heavy stone, narrow streets, iron lampposts burning amber at dusk. The wealthier districts have gas lanterns and clean window glass. The poorer quarters have laundry strung between buildings and children who know to stay out of the way. The river district smells of fish and coal smoke and, in the wrong season, something older beneath it all.

Three institutions share power: a government council, the Church that runs the hospitals, and a Physicians' Guild that licenses medical practice. All three have reasons to control what the city believes about its past. All three have done so, quietly, for twenty years.

The Grey Feast · Twenty Years Ago

The epidemic struck in late autumn. It moved through the poorer quarters first — the river district, the tanneries, the textile slums. By the time wealthy neighborhoods saw it, thousands were dead. Symptoms: a fever indistinguishable from any other, then grey discoloration spreading from the lymph nodes outward in patterns physicians described, in their private notes, as resembling wilted flowers. The Council called it a natural disaster. Physicians who tried to document it found their papers confiscated. At least two doctors were removed from the Guild on charges of "unethical conduct" — a vague accusation requiring no evidence.

Official records say the Grey Feast ended naturally in spring. Older citizens know some quarantine houses were sealed from the outside. That some people went in and didn't come out. That the death toll the Council announced was roughly a third of the true number.

The name "Grey Feast" came from a broadsheet journalist later pressured into retirement. He meant it to describe how the disease consumed people slowly, leaving them hollow and grey-colored before the end. The Council prefers "The Autumn Contagion." Most people, privately, still use his phrase.

Today Viremont wears the face of prosperity — new buildings, a second bridge under construction, a railway station planned for the eastern quarter. The newspapers speak of the future. Everyone has quietly agreed not to speak of the past. This is the city Elias Verne works in. This is the city Eliza Laurent has never left. And twenty years after its first nightmare, it is about to have another.

Section II

Characters

Who they are, and why they can't leave each other.

Protagonist · Detective
Elias Verne
Age 28 · Survivor of the Grey Feast
Appearance Thin, ink-stained hands, clothes that were expensive two years ago and are now merely presentable. He forgets to eat. The dark circles under his eyes look permanent, like they're part of him now.
Manner Watches people the way a physician watches symptoms — cataloguing, waiting for the thing that doesn't fit. He's not cold. He just learned to keep the warmth somewhere the case can't reach it. He's also, occasionally, very funny in the driest possible way, at moments that catch you off-guard.
Wound His parents died in the Grey Feast. He was seven and he was there. He's spent twenty years becoming someone who could have done something about it — and the worst part is, he still doesn't know if he's there yet.
Habit Carries a small notebook. Writes things people say — not for cases, but for himself. Lines he doesn't want to lose. He started because of Eliza, who used to say: "If you don't write it down, it's already half-gone."
Flaw Thinks love looks like solving things. Will tear himself apart to fix what's broken for someone else and not notice he's bleeding. Eliza has been quietly pointing this out for years. He has been quietly not listening.
Hidden Layer

Elias doesn't know his mother was treated briefly by Dr. Thorne during the Grey Feast before being moved to the sealed quarantine house. The treatment was starting to work. He finds this in Thorne's notebook late in Chapter Four — and has to sit with the knowledge that the man who could have saved her existed, tried, and was destroyed for it.

Nurse & Field Researcher
Eliza Laurent
Age 27 · Survivor of the Grey Feast
Appearance Brown eyes that have seen too much and chosen to stay warm anyway. Moves quickly, thinks faster. Started wearing longer sleeves three weeks ago. Tells herself it's the cold.
Manner Practical, precise, devastatingly observant. Fixes problems without announcing she fixed them. Doesn't want credit — she wants the problem gone. Has a particular tone she uses when she means both "I hear you" and "I'm not moving from this" simultaneously, and somehow it's more steadying than any comfort.
Wound Her younger sister Margot died in the Grey Feast. Eliza was with her. Held her hand. She has never told Elias what that was like, and he has never asked, and somehow this mutual silence is one of the kindest things they've ever done for each other.
Flaw Carries things alone for too long. Mistakes withholding the truth for protecting someone. Has done this to Elias, and knows she has, and hates it, and does it anyway — because she knows what he'd sacrifice if she didn't.
Hidden Layer · Major Twist

Eliza has known about the conspiracy's second layer for four months before the game begins. Working night shifts in the hospital archives, she found a partial copy of Thorne's findings filed under a mislabeled patient record. She didn't know enough to act — but she knew enough to be afraid. She started investigating quietly, alone. The reason she's been unwell isn't only the Bloom: she was surveilled, and someone poisoned her water source at the nurse's residency six weeks before the game starts. The Bloom accelerated things. But it didn't start them.

The Angel of the Cathedral
Seraphine
Nature Unknown · Age Indeterminate
Appearance White wings that look like they've been rained on. A face neither young nor old. She doesn't glow. She doesn't illuminate the dark. She stands in it with the ease of someone who has never feared it — or who learned not to, which is different.
Manner Economical. Doesn't comfort and doesn't condemn. Offers information with the weight of someone who knows its cost. Her silences carry as much as her words. Occasionally, if you listen carefully, there is something in her voice that sounds — just for a moment — like grief that has had a very long time to settle.
Twist — Who She Is

Seraphine is not an angel in any traditional sense. She is what remains of Dr. Alaric Thorne's last patient — a woman named Sera, who died in the sealed quarantine house, in the room where Thorne administered his experimental treatment in its final hours before the Council arrived and took everything. She was the one it almost saved. Something of her stayed. The game never confirms this. But her hints are always most specific about things only a patient would know. And in one optional late-game moment, Elias notices her hands — and the faint, faded pattern of grey tracery at the wrist, like old watermarks on paper.

Physician · The Ghost in the Archive
Dr. Alaric Thorne
Dead Twelve Years · Age 41 at Death
Background A lower-Guild physician during the Grey Feast. Not wealthy, not connected. Brilliant in the way people without connections are forced to be — he had to prove everything twice, with evidence, because his position gave him no authority to coast on.
Discovery He identified the causative agent of the Grey Feast, developed a treatment protocol, and had living proof it worked before the Council confiscated his research. He was expelled from the Guild. Eight years later he died of causes his death certificate calls "natural." Two people who knew him believe that.
Twist — Something Remains

Thorne's notebooks contain more than medical notes. In the margins of the final pages, in a different ink — written later, under different light — are annotations that respond to his own text as though in conversation with it. Some of these notes reference details about the sealed quarantine house that Thorne couldn't have known when he wrote the original entries. He wasn't alone when he wrote those margin notes. Someone was with him. The game never names who. But the handwriting in the margins matches the patient intake forms — specifically, the form signed by a woman named Sera, who checked herself into the monastery hospital one week before the Council arrived.

Primary Antagonist · Councilor
Councilor Harwick
Age 62 · A Man Who Told Himself a Story
Function Not a monster. A man who made a decision twenty years ago and spent twenty years justifying it. He genuinely believed that revealing the true scale of the outbreak would cause a financial panic that would kill more people than the disease. He was wrong. He knows it now. He will not say so.
Present When the Bloom returns, Harwick's first instinct is suppression — not from malice, but from the reflexive self-preservation of someone who has built their entire identity on a lie. Exposing the truth would not just end his career. It would make his whole life a crime scene.
Twist — Personal Connection

Harwick's own daughter died in the Grey Feast — in the first week, before he had made any of his decisions. He has spent twenty years telling himself that everything he did afterward was to prevent other fathers from losing their daughters. He even believes it, some days. What Elias eventually realizes is that Harwick's suppression of Thorne's research wasn't purely political — it was also an act of grief, a man so unable to face that a cure existed and arrived too late that he destroyed the evidence of it. This doesn't excuse what he did. But it explains it, and the game asks whether understanding and forgiveness are the same thing.

New · Plot Architecture

The Three Hidden Layers

These are the revelations threaded through the original storyline. Each one recontextualizes what came before it.

Twist One · Revealed in Chapter Three

Eliza Knew First

Four months before Petyr Haas dies in the river district, Eliza found a misfiled patient record containing fragments of Thorne's research. She didn't recognize it for what it was immediately — but she began quietly pulling threads. When the Bloom returned, she already understood more than she let on. She didn't tell Elias because she knew he would stop everything to protect her. She was right. Her notebooks — the ones she eventually gives him — contain her own investigation, running parallel to his, sometimes ahead of it. The woman he thought he was protecting was, in certain chapters, protecting him from knowing how much danger they were both already in.

Twist Two · Revealed in Chapter Four

Seraphine Was Thorne's Patient

Her hints are accurate not because she is supernatural, but because she lived through it. She was in the monastery hospital when Thorne administered his treatment. She was the one it almost saved. Something of her remained — whether as memory, as spirit, or as a dying woman who chose not to let go of the place where she nearly lived, the game leaves open. What closes: the detail about her hands, in one optional scene. The faint grey watermark pattern at the wrist. Elias sees it. He doesn't say anything. He goes home and writes it down in his notebook. The player can decide what they believe.

Twist Three · Revealed in Chapter Four

Thorne Treated Elias's Mother

In the final pages of Thorne's notebook — page 41 of 43 — there is a patient entry: a woman named Mira Verne, river district, admitted in the third week of the Grey Feast, early-stage presentation. Thorne's treatment protocol was administered. She responded. On the next line, in smaller script: transferred, per Council order, to the northern quarantine house. Treatment could not be continued. Elias reads this. He reads it once, sets the notebook down, and is quiet for a very long time. Eliza, beside him, does not say anything. She just stays. This is the scene the whole game was building to, and it contains almost no dialogue.

Section III–VIII

Chapters & Story Beats

Prologue The Grey Feast

The game opens in silence. Not the comfortable kind — the particular silence of a building where everyone is trying not to cough. You are seven years old. The church shelter smells of candle smoke and fear and the broth someone is ladling out near the door. Desaturated colors. Soft edges. The visual grammar of memory.

You find a girl sitting alone near the wall, knees drawn up, watching the room. She has a blanket slightly too big for her. You sit beside her because something in her stillness looks like it's covering something, and you are seven and you understand this without words.

You search the shelter together. You find names on lists. One of them is Margot Laurent. You can show her or not. Either way, she already knows somewhere underneath the searching. She stops at a window and says nothing for a long time.

That night they sit together under their blankets. Elias's is blue — a faded blue the game will reference one more time, at the very end. At some point she falls asleep against his shoulder. He stays very still so he doesn't wake her.

Before the screen fades to black: "No one should have to die alone."

Chapter One The Return of the Bloom

Twenty years later. Elias at his desk at half-past midnight, annotating a death report in red ink: "inconsistent with stated cause," underlined twice.

A dock worker is found near the river district. The attending physician uses the phrase "unusual skin presentation" three times rather than describing what the skin actually looks like. What it looks like is this: grey-black tracery spreading from the lymph nodes, branching like the veins of a dying leaf. The physician wants to write "cardiac event." He's being pressured to. He hasn't signed it yet.

Sister Camille — a nurse who survived the Grey Feast and spent twenty years waiting for it to come back — looks at the body for a long time. Then she goes to find Eliza.

New beat: Elias finds Eliza's handwriting in the hospital margin notes — cross-referencing symptoms in a way that suggests she's been watching this longer than anyone knew. He notices. He doesn't ask yet.

Chapter Two What the Archives Hide

The investigation deepens. The present outbreak and the old one aren't separate events — they're the same event, resumed. The Bloom was never eradicated. It was misdocumented and buried under paperwork, waiting in the walls of quarantine houses and river mud for twenty years.

In the archive: the true death toll, systematically falsified. Instructions from the Council, routed through the Guild. Eight physicians who refused to falsify documents and were subsequently struck from the roster. Among them: Dr. Alaric Thorne.

Elias is followed home and beaten. He wakes on the floor of the Cathedral of Saint Maren. Looks up at the vaulted ceiling. And then — she is simply there. Seraphine. White wings. A face that gives away almost nothing. New: he notices, in this first meeting, that she holds her hands a certain careful way — like someone who learned not to let people see them. He doesn't know why he notices this.

She gives him two hints. He uses them carefully. He finds the monastery hospital.

Chapter Three The Secret She Kept

He finds the medicine in her desk drawer, and the handkerchief, and the notebook — her parallel investigation, going back months before his began. He reads only the first line of the last entry before he closes it, because the rest belongs to her.

The first line: "He's going to be angry with me. He's going to be wrong to be angry with me. I hope he figures that out before he says something he'll regret."

She comes home. He's at the window with his back to the room, which is what he does when he's not ready to be looked at yet.

New layer: when she finally tells him everything — including that someone had been watching her, that the water was poisoned, that she'd been carrying this alone for four months — his anger is real. But so is his recognition that she was right. That if he'd known, he would have made her the case. She knows him too well for it to be a guess. He hates this. She knows he hates it. Neither of them says so.

Chapter Four The Weight of Truth

The city is in controlled panic. Elias assembles the cure from Thorne's scattered materials. Eliza works alongside him with whatever energy she has left, which is less than last month and more than it will be next month, and she puts it toward the work because the work is what she's always had.

Page 41 of Thorne's notebook. Mira Verne. Treatment administered. Responding. Transferred, per Council order, to the northern quarantine house. Treatment could not be continued.

Elias sets the notebook down. He is quiet for a very long time. Eliza stays.

Then the confrontation with Harwick. Then the truth about Harwick's daughter. Then the choice: exposure first, or cure first, or both — and the game begins tracking which path you take.

Seraphine appears one final time, in the Cathedral, briefly. She says almost nothing. But Elias asks her about the monastery hospital — and she pauses in a way that is not quite human. Then she says: "Someone was kind to me there, once." She's gone before he can ask anything else.

Rewritten · Key Scenes

Dialogue — Naturalized

The goal: what they don't say matters as much as what they do. Interruptions, silences, and small humor live alongside the heavy things.

The Hospital · Nurse's Washroom Chapter One · Early Morning

She's washing her hands at the basin. She knows his footsteps — has known them for twenty years, the particular weight of someone who moves like he's always about to stop and write something down. She doesn't look up right away.

Eliza
The dock worker.
Elias
Sister Camille found you first.
Eliza
She didn't need to say anything. I've been watching her face for twenty years. I know what it looks like when she recognizes something she's been dreading. (beat) You need to see the body.
Elias
Already cleared it with the attending physician?
Eliza
He was relieved, actually. I think he's been standing next to it hoping someone more certain than him would show up.
Elias
That's a very diplomatic way of describing cowardice.
Eliza
He's not a coward. He's just been told what to write and he hasn't written it yet, which is about as brave as most people get. (drying her hands, reaching for her coat) Come on. The more time we spend here, the more time someone has to move the paperwork.

He follows her. He notices, in the doorway, that she pauses for just a moment before pulling her coat on — the way you do when a motion costs more than it should. He doesn't say anything. He files it.

Hospital Corridor Chapter Two · Late Night

He finds her half-asleep in the corridor, notebook open on her knee. He sits down beside her the way he did twenty years ago in a church shelter. Without asking. Without making it into anything.

For a while, neither of them speaks. There's a clock somewhere. There's the distant sound of someone two floors up, moving carts.

Eliza
Do you remember what color my blanket was? In the shelter.
Elias
Brown. Too big. You kept getting the edge caught under your feet and then stepping on it and nearly tripping.
Eliza
(small smile) Good.
Elias
Checking what, exactly?
Eliza
That you remember it the same way I do. (pause) Never mind. It's late.
Elias
You're being cryptic at — (checks his watch) — half past one. That's unlike you.
Eliza
I'm being tired. There's a difference.

He doesn't push. He just stays. After a while, she leans her head back against the wall, and the notebook almost slides off her knee, and he catches it without thinking and sets it beside her without reading it, because that's not something he'd do.

The player doesn't understand why she was checking. Yet.

Eliza's Apartment Chapter Three · Evening

He's at the window, back to the room. She comes in without knocking — she never knocks, she has a key — and stops.

Elias
How long?
Eliza
The illness, or the investigation I've been running separately from yours?
Elias
(turning) ...Both, apparently.
Eliza
Illness — a month, maybe more. The investigation — four months. I found a misfiled patient record in the archive. Fragments of Thorne's research. I didn't know what it was at first, but I—
Elias
Four months. You've known for four months and you've been working next to me and you didn't—
Eliza
Because you would have stopped.
Elias
I would have found the cure—
Eliza
You would have found my cure. And left everyone else's for later. And you know that.

Silence. He does know it. That's the problem.

Elias
Someone poisoned your water supply. Someone was watching you. You were dealing with that alone for—
Eliza
I wasn't alone. I had Sister Camille. And I had enough of your methods memorized to do it carefully.
Elias
That is not the same thing.
Eliza
No. It isn't. (quietly) And I'm sorry for that part. I'm not sorry for the rest of it.

He turns back to the window. She doesn't fill the silence. She knows him well enough to know he needs to run it through to the end before he can be in the same room with it.

Elias
You taught me once that every life matters equally. That there's no hierarchy of grief.
Eliza
I remember.
Elias
You taught me that when we were seven years old. In a shelter. With people dying around us.
Eliza
Elias.
Elias
I'm still angry.
Eliza
I know.
Elias
That's all I wanted to say.
Eliza
Okay. (pause) Are you going to make tea, or should I?

He almost laughs. It's not quite a laugh. But it's something. He goes to put the kettle on.

Cathedral of Saint Maren Chapter Two · First Meeting with Seraphine

He's on the floor near the altar. He doesn't know how he got here. His eye is swollen shut. The candlelight is just enough to see by. He's lying very still because moving seems inadvisable. Then — she is simply there. She doesn't arrive. She's present in the way certain silences are present.

Seraphine
You're not dead.
Elias
I had gathered that. Thank you.
Seraphine
You sound irritated.
Elias
I'm trying to assess whether I have a concussion, which requires me to follow a moving point of light and I don't currently have a moving point of light. You could help with that, if you're amenable.
Seraphine
(pause) Your pupils are even. You have a headache and two cracked ribs, not a concussion. You'll need to breathe shallowly for a week.
Elias
That was a specific diagnosis for someone standing that far away.
Seraphine
I know what the illness looks like. I know what the injury looks like. They're different.

He sits up slowly. This is a mistake. He does it anyway.

Elias
What are you?
Seraphine
Something that was here. Something that stayed. (pause) I can give you two answers to two questions. After that I can't help you further. Choose carefully when you ask them.
Elias
Why two?
Seraphine
That's one.
Elias
(closing his eyes) Fair enough.

When he opens his eyes, she's gone. He sits in the silence for a while. Then he gets up, very carefully, and goes to find Eliza — because this is, apparently, what he does.

On his way out he pauses. Looks back at the spot where she stood. There is, on the cold stone floor, the faintest water stain in a shape that looks — just for a moment — like the shadow a wing makes. He writes it down. He moves on.

Councilor Harwick's Office Chapter Four · Late Afternoon

Elias puts the evidence on the desk. He does it without drama — each piece following from the last, a quiet sequence. He doesn't sit down. He stands and lets the silence do some of the work. Harwick looks at the instruction letter with his own signature on it. Twenty years old. Still perfectly legible.

Harwick
You don't understand what governance requires—
Elias
You're right that I've never governed anything. I'd like to keep it that way. What I do understand is that a physician found a treatment that worked, and you buried him, and my mother was one of the patients who would have lived if you hadn't. So I'm not the right person to explain to about what governance requires.

That lands. Harwick looks at him — really looks, for the first time — and something shifts in his expression. Not guilt, exactly. Something older than guilt.

Harwick
My daughter died in the first week. Before any of it. Before the decision. She was nine years old and I was in my office at the Council chamber when— (stops) I'm not telling you this as an excuse.
Elias
I know.
Harwick
I told myself everything I did afterward was to make sure no one else— that it would mean something, that her—
Elias
Councilor. (quietly) I know. And I'm genuinely sorry about your daughter. But that knowledge — that you were in grief, that you believed what you were doing — that doesn't undo the instruction letter. It explains it. Those aren't the same thing.

Harwick is quiet for a long time. He looks out the window at the city — the one he helped rebuild, the one that is quietly dying again of the same thing.

Harwick
What do you want?
Elias
Quarantine protocols updated with the correct transmission information. Thorne's treatment authorized for immediate use under his name. The Guild physicians freed from suppression orders. And a full accounting — what happened twenty years ago, what was decided, and who decided it — in writing, signed, submitted to the public record. (pause) That's all.
Harwick
That will end my career.
Elias
Yes.
Harwick
(long pause) I thought I was protecting the city.
Elias
I know.
Harwick
That doesn't—
Elias
No. It doesn't. (he picks up his notebook) I'll leave the copies. The originals go to the broadsheet. I'd suggest you have your lawyer present when you read them.

He leaves. In the hallway outside, he stops. Takes a slow breath. Writes one line in his notebook. He doesn't show the player what it says. But the camera lingers long enough to see his handwriting, steady and precise, filling a small square of white paper.

Eliza's Apartment Final Days · The Notebook Scene

She's lucid. There are hours of clarity now, patches of herself she uses deliberately. She puts the notebook in his hands and holds his hands around it for a moment — one of the few times she touches him on purpose.

Eliza
You've spent twenty years carrying everyone. Being the one who remembers, who investigates, who stays.
Elias
Eliza—
Eliza
I'm not done. (he closes his mouth) I want you to have something to carry that isn't a case. Even if it's only paper. Even if it's only words. It counts. Okay?
Elias
Okay.
Eliza
Don't read it until the case is finished.
Elias
Eliza—
Eliza
I know. (gently) Finish it first. Then read it. And then — don't stay because of me. Don't let me be a reason you stop.
Elias
That's not—
Eliza
Elias. I know you. Make it possible.

He doesn't say anything for a while. He's looking at the notebook. His handwriting on cases. Her handwriting on everything else.

Elias
You could have told me. At seven. That you were scared. I would have — I didn't know what to do but I would have tried.
Eliza
You sat down next to me. That was enough.
Elias
(barely audible) It wasn't.
Eliza
It was. It was exactly enough. (pause) Go finish it.

Section IX

All Endings

The endings are not rewards or punishments. They are consequences. The game tracks not just what you accomplish, but how — with what care, what priorities, what understanding of what the story has been about.

Ending One The Physician Best Ending
Requirements
  • Complete all components of Thorne's treatment protocol
  • Collect and preserve the full evidence against the Council
  • Use Seraphine's hints deliberately, not desperately
  • Never skip Eliza's scenes to rush the investigation
  • Discover the truth about Elias's mother (page 41)

The Bloom is contained. Not quickly — weeks more of work, training physicians, fighting institutional resistance. The final death wave is smaller than it should have been. Not small enough. Never small enough. But smaller.

Harwick resigns in a formal proceeding the newspapers call historic. The Guild is freed. The true death toll is counted for the first time. The city begins the long, painful process of knowing what actually happened to it.

Elias publishes Thorne's protocol under Thorne's name. He is not legally required to. He does it anyway.

Epilogue: Elias at a larger desk, in a building with the quiet gravity of an institution. The door behind him: The Laurent Institute for Medical Research. Her notebook open on the desk. His tea going cold.

"The cure came too late for one life. But not for the rest."
Ending Two The Truth Accountability Path
Requirements
  • Expose the Council's conspiracy fully and publicly before the cure is complete
  • Miss some components of the cure — investigation pushed toward exposure over treatment
  • Build the evidentiary case rigorously but prioritize accountability over containment

The exposure happens first. The story breaks before the treatment is finished, and the city's reaction is not calm or orderly. Riots in the wealthy quarters, silent weeping crowds in the river district. The anger has been waiting twenty years. It doesn't express itself neatly.

More people die during the additional weeks before the treatment is completed by a Guild collaboration working from Thorne's notes. Elias counts them, the way he counts everything. He writes down the number. He knows what it means in the economy of choices he made.

Harwick faces formal charges. The city goes through a public reckoning that is painful and incomplete and necessary. Elias testifies. He declines every appointment offered to him. He goes home and reads Eliza's notebook.

"Truth does not resurrect the dead. But perhaps it prevents new graves from being dug in silence."
Ending Three Bloom Eternal Failure State
Requirements
  • Fail to complete the investigation before the outbreak accelerates
  • Make wrong deductions that mislead treatment development
  • Neglect quarantine protocols due to insufficient evidence-gathering

Too little, too late. Not because Elias didn't try. But grief made him imprecise in the fourth chapter, and imprecision in investigation is the thing that can't be undone.

Viremont doesn't collapse entirely — cities rarely do. But it becomes a diminished thing. Emptier streets. Bells more common than commerce. The wealthy leave first, as the wealthy always do. The poor stay, as the poor always do, and bury their dead with the efficiency of people who have learned not to have ceremonies about something this regular.

Elias stays. He walks the empty streets in the evenings and sometimes — not always — talks to Eliza. Not because he's confused. He knows. Some habits you don't break. You just carry them differently.

"I solved the mystery. I found every answer. I was right about all of it." / "I was too late."
Ending Four The Angel's Silence The Question Path
Requirements
  • Use both of Seraphine's hints early and rely on them for key deductions
  • Achieve a good outcome, but the method reveals a dependency on the inexplicable
  • Investigate Seraphine instead of leaving the mystery alone

The disease is contained. The conspiracy exposed, more or less. By the metrics of outcomes, this looks like success — and in many ways it is. But Elias can't leave the Cathedral alone. He goes back looking for Seraphine the way people look for something they've lost — methodically, without admitting they're looking.

One final visit. He stands where he first saw her. Waits for a long time. Nothing comes. Then — so quietly he's not certain he doesn't imagine it — a voice: "Does it matter?"

Just the candles and the cold and the city carrying on. He reads Eliza's notebook. In one of her margins: "Some things are real without being explainable. You're going to want to argue with me about this. Don't."

"Some things are just there — real, and given, and not requiring our understanding to be true."
Secret Ending The Promise 100% Completion
Requirements
  • 100% completion — every evidence piece found
  • Every memory fragment and childhood flashback viewed
  • Every page of Eliza's notebook read before the final chapter
  • Discover all three hidden layers (Eliza's investigation, Seraphine's identity, Elias's mother)
  • Choose the dialogue option that keeps his promise to her in every scene where it appears
  • Complete both the cure and the exposure

After the credits of whichever main ending you've reached. Time has passed. Elias looks older — not dramatically, just in the way people look older when they've been living rather than merely enduring.

He returns to Saint Maren's Church — not the Cathedral where he met Seraphine, but the original shelter where he and Eliza spent that autumn. The building is warm now. Community house. Children's drawings pinned by the windows. Someone painted the walls a soft yellow.

He sits in the corner where they sat that night. A child of about eight comes and sits beside him, without asking, without ceremony — the way curious children do.

Child
Were you scared? When you were here before — when the bad sickness was happening?
Elias
Terrified.
Child
Then why did you stay?

He thinks of a girl with a blanket too big for her. Who sat down beside her own grief and didn't look away from it. Who held her sister's hand. Who, twenty years later, held onto clarity long enough to say: don't stay because of me. Finish it.

Elias
Because once, someone stayed for me.

The camera lifts. Through the window, above the rooftops, in pale morning air — a white feather, drifting up until the sky takes it. No dramatic music. The screen fades — not to black. To white.

No final text. The menu screen reloads — and this time, it shows the church shelter at dawn, warm light through old windows, two small figures sitting together against the wall. The player understands, for the first time, that they have been watching this ending since the beginning.

"No one should have to die alone."

Section X

What the Game Is Really About

People think the game is about a detective solving a plague. It is about what it costs to see clearly in a world that would rather not be seen. And about two people who, at seven years old, decided to see it together — and never really stopped.

🕯
Fear spreads faster than disease

The Bloom kills dozens. The panic and suppression around it kill hundreds. Every time the Council suppresses information, you can trace the downstream deaths. Silence is not safety. Silence is a different kind of contagion.

📄
Truth decays differently than you'd expect

It doesn't disappear — it waits in monastery basements and old notebooks and the faces of survivors. Twenty years is a long time to keep a lie intact. Eventually the lie itself, resuming, cracks it open.

⚗️
Progress is cumulative

Thorne is the game's quiet hero — a man who never appears, who existed only in his notes, who was erased from official history and still left something that could save people after his death. Every patient saved in the better endings is a patient Thorne saved, twenty years late.

🤝
The love that needs no confession

Elias and Eliza never say "I love you." They say "you forgot to eat" and "I cleared it with the physician" and "I was checking." Their relationship is not incomplete. It is a different kind of complete.

⚖️
Grief and guilt are not the same thing

Elias's grief drives him toward compulsive thoroughness. Eliza's drives her toward compulsive care for others. Harwick's grief drove him toward a decision that killed thousands. The game tracks all three and asks what you do with the grief you're given.

🌿
A promise as a life

"No one should have to die alone." Elias made this promise at seven and has spent twenty years fulfilling it in ways he doesn't call keeping a promise because he doesn't know any other way to be. The Secret Ending suggests a person can be a promise — if someone else helps carry it.

✦

The game's final thesis — visible only in the Secret Ending, and only to those who have paid attention throughout — is this: Elias came back to the shelter to sit where he once sat. He made room beside him, and someone sat there. He passed forward the thing Eliza gave him without knowing that's what he was doing. The promise is not a weight. It is a door that keeps opening.
